## Intuition

A projection casts a vector's shadow onto a line or plane, as if a light were shining straight down perpendicular to that subspace. The shadow is the closest point on the subspace to the original vector - projecting is how you find "the best approximation of v that lives on this line."

## Formal definition

The{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="projection" pronunciation="proj sub u of v" meaning="the closest point to v lying on the line spanned by u">$\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{u}}(\vec{v})$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
of $\vec{v}$ onto the line spanned by $\vec{u}$ is:

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\vec{u}}(\vec{v}) = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{u}} \, \vec{u}
$$

The **residual** $\vec{v} - \operatorname{proj}_{\vec{u}}(\vec{v})$ is always orthogonal to $\vec{u}$ - that perpendicularity is exactly what makes the projection the *closest* point on the line, not just some point on it.

This generalizes to projecting onto a higher-dimensional subspace spanned by an orthonormal basis $\{\vec{e}_1, \ldots, \vec{e}_k\}$: sum the projections onto each basis vector, $\sum_i (\vec{e}_i \cdot \vec{v})\,\vec{e}_i$.

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  The subspace-projection formula $\sum_i (\vec{e}_i \cdot \vec{v})\,\vec{e}_i$ only works directly when the $\vec{e}_i$ are orthonormal. Projecting onto a subspace spanned by a non-orthogonal basis needs the more general least-squares formula instead.
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  A projection is not generally invertible - many different vectors $\vec{v}$ can share the same shadow on the line, so you can't recover $\vec{v}$ from its projection alone.
</Callout>

## Intuition

QR decomposition replaces a matrix's columns with an orthonormal basis $Q$ that spans the same space, plus an upper-triangular matrix $R$ recording how to rebuild the original columns from that basis. It's the algebraic form of the Gram-Schmidt process: straighten out a set of vectors into mutually perpendicular, unit-length directions, one at a time, without changing what they span.

## Formal definition

For a matrix{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="QR decomposition" pronunciation="A equals Q R" meaning="factoring A into an orthonormal Q and upper-triangular R">$A = QR$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
with independent columns, $Q$ has orthonormal columns and $R$ is upper-triangular with positive diagonal entries.

**Gram-Schmidt** builds $Q$ one column at a time: normalize the first column, then for each subsequent column, subtract off its projections onto all previously built $q_i$'s before normalizing:

$$
q_k = \frac{a_k - \sum_{i<k} (q_i \cdot a_k)\, q_i}{\left\lVert a_k - \sum_{i<k} (q_i \cdot a_k)\, q_i \right\rVert}
$$

$R$'s entries are exactly those projection coefficients: $R_{ik} = q_i \cdot a_k$ for $i \le k$.

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  Classical Gram-Schmidt is numerically unstable in floating point - columns that are nearly dependent can lose orthogonality after rounding error accumulates. Practical implementations use modified Gram-Schmidt or Householder reflections instead.
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  QR decomposition requires the columns of $A$ to be linearly independent to produce a full-rank $Q$ - if a column is a combination of earlier ones, Gram-Schmidt subtracts it down to the zero vector and normalizing breaks down.
</Callout>

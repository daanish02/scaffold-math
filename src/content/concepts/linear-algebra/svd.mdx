## Intuition

Every matrix - even a non-square one, even a singular one - can be broken into three simple moves: a rotation, a scaling along perpendicular axes, and another rotation. No matter how it distorts space, a matrix always sends the unit circle to an ellipse, and the SVD tells you exactly where that ellipse's axes point and how long they are. Those axis lengths, the **singular values**, measure how much the matrix stretches space in its most and least sensitive directions.

## Formal definition

Every $m \times n$ matrix{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="SVD" pronunciation="A equals U Sigma V transpose" meaning="factoring any matrix into rotation, scaling, rotation">$A = U\Sigma V^T$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
factors as:

- $V$ ($n \times n$, orthogonal) - rotates the input space so its axes align with the directions $A$ treats specially.
- $\Sigma$ ($m \times n$, diagonal, nonnegative, descending) - scales along those axes by the **singular values** $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0$.
- $U$ ($m \times m$, orthogonal) - rotates the result into the output space.

Unlike eigendecomposition, SVD exists for *every* matrix - rectangular, singular, anything - because $U$ and $V$ are allowed to be different rotations, rather than requiring the matrix to be diagonalizable in its own basis. The singular values relate to eigenvalues via $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^TA)}$, and the columns of $V$ are the corresponding eigenvectors of $A^TA$.

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  SVD is not the same as eigendecomposition, even for a square matrix - $U$ and $V$ can differ (and usually do, unless $A$ is symmetric positive semidefinite), while eigendecomposition reuses one basis for both "sides" of the matrix.
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  Singular values are always nonnegative, even when the matrix's eigenvalues are negative or complex - $\sigma_i$ measures a *scale factor*, which can never be negative, unlike an eigenvalue.
</Callout>

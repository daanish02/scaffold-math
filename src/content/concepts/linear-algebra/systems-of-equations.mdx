## Intuition

A system of linear equations asks: where do these lines (or planes, in higher dimensions) all cross at once? Each equation draws one line; the solution is the point every line agrees on. Written as $Ax = b$, the same question becomes "which input $x$ does $A$ send to $b$?" - solving the system is undoing the transformation.

## Formal definition

A system of $m$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns is written compactly as{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="linear system" pronunciation="A x equals b" meaning="matrix equation encoding m equations in n unknowns">$Ax = b$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
, where $A$ is $m \times n$, $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the unknown vector, and $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

**Gaussian elimination** reduces $A$ to row-echelon form via row operations (swap, scale, add a multiple of one row to another) that preserve the solution set, then back-substitutes to recover $x$.

A system has exactly one of three outcomes:

- **Unique solution** - the rows of $A$ are independent and span enough directions to pin down one point.
- **No solution** - the equations are inconsistent (e.g. two parallel lines that never meet).
- **Infinitely many solutions** - the equations are redundant, leaving a free direction (e.g. two equations describing the same line).

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  "No solution" and "infinitely many solutions" both come from rows becoming all-zero during elimination - the difference is whether the corresponding entry in $b$ is also zero. All-zero row with nonzero $b$-entry means inconsistent; all-zero row with zero $b$-entry means a free variable.
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  More equations than unknowns doesn't guarantee a unique solution, and fewer doesn't guarantee infinitely many - what matters is the rank of $A$, not just its shape.
</Callout>

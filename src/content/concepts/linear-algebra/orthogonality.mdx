## Intuition

Two vectors are orthogonal when they point in directions that meet at a right angle - like the x-axis and y-axis. There's no need to measure the angle with a protractor: a single algebraic test, the dot product, tells you instantly. When the dot product of two vectors is zero, they're perpendicular; no other value of the dot product gives such a clean geometric fact.

## Visual / interactive

Drag either point (treating it as a vector from the origin). The two vectors shown start perpendicular - as you move them, watch how the angle between them changes. Their dot product $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2$ is zero exactly when they're at a right angle; positive when the angle is acute, negative when obtuse.

## Formal definition

Two vectors{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="orthogonal" pronunciation="u perp v" meaning="vectors meeting at a right angle, with zero dot product">$\vec{u} \perp \vec{v}$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
are **orthogonal** if $\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0$.

This follows from the general relationship between the dot product and the angle $\theta$ between two vectors:

$$
\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = \lVert \vec{u} \rVert \, \lVert \vec{v} \rVert \cos\theta
$$

Since $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$, orthogonality is exactly the case where this product vanishes (for nonzero vectors).

A set of vectors is **orthonormal** if every pair is orthogonal *and* every vector has unit length ($\lVert \vec{v}_i \rVert = 1$) - the natural generalization of the standard basis axes.

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector (its dot product with anything is 0), but it has no direction - don't read "orthogonal" as implying two well-defined, nonzero directions unless you've checked both vectors are nonzero.
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  Orthogonal and orthonormal aren't the same: orthogonal only requires the right angle, while orthonormal also requires unit length. Two perpendicular vectors of length 5 and 3 are orthogonal but not orthonormal.
</Callout>

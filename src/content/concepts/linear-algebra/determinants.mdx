## Intuition

The determinant is a single number that tells you how a matrix scales area (in 2D) or volume (in 3D and beyond). A unit square has area 1; after applying a matrix $A$, it becomes a parallelogram with area $|\det A|$. If the determinant is negative, the transformation also flips orientation (like a mirror). If it's zero, the transformation squashes the plane down to a line or point - information is lost and the matrix can't be undone.

## Formal definition

For a $2\times2$ matrix{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="determinant" pronunciation="det A" meaning="signed scale factor a matrix applies to area or volume">$\det A$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
, $\det \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc$. For larger matrices, the determinant is defined recursively by cofactor expansion along any row or column.

**Key properties**:

- $\det A = 0$ if and only if $A$ is singular (not invertible) - its columns are linearly dependent.
- $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ - scale factors multiply when transformations compose.
- $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$, since undoing a transformation undoes its scale factor too.
- Swapping two rows flips the sign of the determinant.

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  A zero determinant means "not invertible," not "the zero matrix." Plenty of nonzero matrices have determinant zero - any matrix whose columns are linearly dependent, like [[1,2],[2,4]].
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  $\det(A + B) \neq \det A + \det B$ in general - the determinant is not linear over matrix addition, only multiplicative over matrix products.
</Callout>

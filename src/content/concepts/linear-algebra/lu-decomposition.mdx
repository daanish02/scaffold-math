## Intuition

LU decomposition packages Gaussian elimination into two triangular matrices: $L$ (lower-triangular, recording the row-elimination multipliers) and $U$ (upper-triangular, the result of elimination). Once you have $A = LU$, solving $Ax = b$ for many different $b$'s becomes cheap - you only redo two easy triangular solves each time, instead of re-running elimination from scratch.

## Formal definition

For a square matrix{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="LU decomposition" pronunciation="A equals L U" meaning="factoring A into a lower-triangular L and upper-triangular U">$A = LU$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
, $L$ is lower-triangular with 1's on the diagonal and $U$ is upper-triangular, obtained by recording the row multipliers used during Gaussian elimination.

Once factored, solving $Ax = b$ becomes two triangular solves:

$$
Ly = b \quad \text{(forward substitution)}, \qquad Ux = y \quad \text{(back substitution)}
$$

Each triangular solve costs $O(n^2)$ instead of the $O(n^3)$ cost of elimination - so factoring once and solving for many $b$'s is far cheaper than re-eliminating each time.

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  Not every matrix has a plain LU decomposition - a zero pivot can force a row swap, which needs a permutation matrix $P$, giving $PA = LU$ instead. Most practical LU solvers include this pivoting by default.
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  Factoring $A = LU$ once doesn't help if $A$ itself changes between solves - the cost savings only apply across multiple right-hand sides $b$ for the *same* $A$.
</Callout>

## Intuition

A 2x2 matrix can be read as an instruction for moving every point in the plane at once - stretching, squashing, rotating, or flipping space. Some directions survive this move unchanged in orientation, only scaled: those are the interesting ones. An eigenvector points along one of those surviving directions, and its eigenvalue is how much it gets stretched or shrunk.

## Formal definition

For a square matrix{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="matrix" pronunciation="A" meaning="a square array of numbers representing a linear map">$A$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
, a nonzero vector{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="eigenvector" pronunciation="v" meaning="a direction left unrotated by A, only scaled">$v$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
is an **eigenvector** with **eigenvalue**{" "}
<SymbolTooltip client:visible name="eigenvalue" pronunciation="lambda" meaning="the scale factor applied along an eigenvector's direction">$\lambda$</SymbolTooltip>{" "}
if:

$$
Av = \lambda v
$$

For a 2x2 matrix, eigenvalues solve the characteristic equation $\lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0$ - equivalently, $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.

## Common pitfalls

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  Not every real matrix has real eigenvectors - a rotation matrix, for example, has none (its eigenvalues are complex), since rotation doesn't leave any real direction unrotated.
</Callout>

<Callout label="Common pitfall">
  Eigenvectors are only defined up to scale - any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector for the same eigenvalue. There's no single "the" eigenvector for a given eigenvalue, only a line of them.
</Callout>
